## Scope {#s-scope}

The student, teacher and researcher policies should be read together. They address different roles but are based on the same principles of transparency, responsibility, and academic integrity.

This policy governs the use of genAI tools, such as ChatGPT, NotebookLM, and Copilot, for students enrolled at the SRC. Existing rules regarding plagiarism and academic misconduct apply.

Regardless of whether the use is allowed, you should be aware of the limitations of chatbots (hallucinations, bias, etc.), which are not within the scope of this document. Understanding AI and its limitations is your responsibility.

By employing genAI tools, students commit to adhering to the following principles:

## 1. Acceptable use {#s-1}

Students may use generative AI tools to support their learning and their own academic work. For example, genAI may be used to explain concepts, support discussion and reflection, provide feedback on student-produced work, and assist with language, clarity, or presentation.

GenAI must not substitute for the student’s own performance of the knowledge, skills, reasoning, analysis, writing, coding, modelling, or other intellectual work being assessed. Students must therefore ensure that submitted work primarily represents their own intellectual contribution.

## 2. Responsibility and transparency {#s-2}

Students are fully responsible for all material they submit for examination or assessment, including assignments, examinations, theses, models, analyses, and code. This responsibility applies regardless of whether genAI tools have been used.

Where permitted use of genAI has meaningfully influenced submitted work, students must disclose that use. The disclosure should normally state which tool was used, for what purpose, and how its output influenced the submitted work. Disclosure does not in itself make an otherwise prohibited use of genAI permissible.

## 3. Consequences for Academic Misconduct {#s-3}

Use of genAI may constitute academic misconduct when it is inconsistent with this policy, applicable university rules, or instructions for the specific course, assignment, examination, or degree project. This may include, for example, submitting AI-generated or substantially AI-produced work as the student’s own, failing to disclose genAI use where disclosure is required, or using genAI in a context where its use has been explicitly prohibited or restricted. Suspected cases will be handled in accordance with established university procedures for academic misconduct. See [Your rights and responsibilities - Stockholms universitet](https://www.su.se/english/education/student-support/your-rights-and-responsibilities) for more information.

## 4. Additional Considerations {#s-4}

Specific courses may provide additional criteria and more detailed instructions on the use of AI tools. These course-level instructions may also deviate from this general policy, for example, by prohibiting the use of genAI for particular assignments, or by allowing it only for limited purposes such as language correction, concision, or formatting. Students must therefore always follow the instructions provided for the specific course, module, assignment, or examination. Where relevant, course instructions may also distinguish between different forms of work. For example, there may be more flexibility for formative or in-between assignments, where genAI can support learning and revision, while stricter rules may apply to final assessments, examinations, theses, or other work submitted for formal grading.

Student performance is evaluated based on the quality of their degree project in relation to the learning outcomes. Using genAI in accordance with this policy does not affect the grading of the submitted work.

It’s important to note that we also have a policy on the use of AI by teachers. [You can read this policy here (Box PDF)](https://stockholmuniversity.app.box.com/s/zwbmrbvifsh0fsbt68vkys9nrbkar84a).

This document will be regularly reviewed and updated to reflect developments in the field, changes in university policy, and emerging evidence on responsible AI use in education.

## Application of the Policy in Practice {#s-practice}

A helpful way to think about AI tools is to imagine them as a smart but overconfident classmate who answers any questions you send them.

You are allowed to ask this friend questions, discuss ideas, and use those discussions to improve your own understanding and writing. However, just as with a real person, you cannot submit their words, analyses, or solutions as if they were your own work.

The academic rules that apply to human assistance also apply here.

### Acceptable use {#s-acceptable}

Using genAI tools is generally acceptable when they function as support for your own thinking and learning, for example:

- Asking genAI to explain a concept you do not understand, then checking the explanation against reliable sources before writing your own explanation in your own words.
- Discussing how an argument might be structured and then independently deciding what to argue and how.
- Getting feedback on clarity, grammar, or language in a text you have written yourself.
- Asking for suggestions on how to improve a draft and choosing which suggestions (if any) to adopt.

In these cases, you remain the author of the submitted work.

### Unacceptable use {#s-unacceptable}

Problems arise when the “classmate” effectively does the work for you.

This includes situations such as:

- Submitting text, code, or analyses written by the genAI, even if you have lightly edited or paraphrased them.
- Generating citations or references with genAI that you have not personally read and verified.
- Having genAI generate an argument or explanation and then “confirming it” by checking a few websites, without substantially developing the work yourself.
- Having genAI rewrite your entire draft into a “better” version, where your own ideas and structure are largely replaced by the genAI’s choices.

In these cases, your classmate would be considered the author of the submitted work, as it would no longer primarily represent your own intellectual contribution. Presenting such work as your own may constitute academic misconduct.

### Transparency matters {#s-transparency}

If you use AI tools in a way that meaningfully shapes your work, this should be clearly described in your submission. This disclosure is not meant to penalise you but is simply part of academic honesty. Your teachers are expected to do the same.

A simple description of:

- which tool was used,
- for what purpose,
- and how its output influenced your work

is usually sufficient.

## About this policy {#s-about}

This policy was developed by staff at the Stockholm Resilience Centre and discussed with researchers, teachers, and students. Generative AI (Claude Opus 4.6 and ChatGPT 5.5) was used to support drafting, language editing, and text restructuring.
