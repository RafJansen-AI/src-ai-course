## Scope {#t-scope}

The student, teacher and researcher policies should be read together. They address different roles but are based on the same principles of transparency, responsibility, and academic integrity.

This policy governs how teaching staff may use generative AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT, NotebookLM, Copilot and similar) in teaching, supervision, assessment, and student communication. “Teaching staff” includes (as applicable) course coordinators, teachers/lecturers, supervisors, examiners, teaching assistants, and graders. Existing professional, legal, and ethical obligations (e.g., fair assessment, confidentiality, and good pedagogical practice) continue to apply.

Regardless of whether the use is allowed, you should be aware of the limitations of chatbots (hallucinations, bias, etc.), which are not within the scope of this document. Understanding AI and its limitations is your responsibility.

Teaching staff (in particular the course coordinator/examiner) may limit, expand, or specify how students may use genAI tools for each course or assessment, provided this is communicated clearly to students (e.g., in the syllabus, assignment brief, or assessment instructions) and is consistent with university rules. If a course or assignment does not specify any restrictions or permissions, then the default student AI policy applies. [You can read this policy here (Box PDF)](https://stockholmuniversity.app.box.com/s/fpf4hea5v7mfrrtuu8llglevd2uum79a). This document will be regularly reviewed and updated to reflect developments in the field, changes in university policy, and emerging evidence on responsible AI use in education.

By using AI tools in teaching-related work, teaching staff commit to the following principles:

## 1. Authorized use {#t-1}

Teaching staff may use genAI tools to support teaching and learning activities (e.g., brainstorming learning activities, improving clarity of instructions, language editing, generating examples, drafting rubrics or feedback templates). AI tools must not be used in ways that replace core staff responsibilities such as academic judgement, supervision, or student support.

## 2. Transparency and accountability {#t-2}

Teaching staff remain fully responsible for all course materials, feedback, and decisions communicated to students. When genAI tools have been used in producing student-facing content (e.g., feedback, instructions, or messages to students), staff are expected to disclose the type of use clearly and simply (see examples in the application section). This is particularly important to avoid distrust when students suspect AI-written text but cannot determine whether it was lightly edited or fully AI-generated.

## 3. Assessment integrity and student rights {#t-3}

### A. Non-graded activities {#t-3a}

For non-graded feedback or student-facing text (e.g., course materials, formative feedback, learning support, clarifying instructions, email replies), teaching staff may use genAI at their discretion, provided they disclose the type of use (as in point 2) and verify the result before using it.

### B. Graded work {#t-3b}

For graded assignments, exams, theses, or any assessment that determines learning outcomes, the grade and evaluative judgement must be determined solely by teaching staff. GenAI tools must not be used to propose, recommend, justify, compare, or otherwise influence the grade (directly or indirectly). This constitutes high-risk use under the EU AI Act. See [EU AI Act Annex 3](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/annex-3). After the grade decision is made, AI tools may be used only for language/clarity edits of written comments, without changing the substantive evaluation.

### C. Regarding AI detection software {#t-3c}

Teaching staff must not use AI-detection tools to infer whether student writing was AI-generated. These tools are too unreliable, and their use would constitute high-risk use under the EU AI Act. See [EU AI Act Annex 3](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/annex-3)

## 4. Confidentiality, data protection, and misuse {#t-4}

Teaching staff must not paste or upload sensitive student data (e.g., health information, disciplinary matters, unpublished theses, etc.) into AI tools unless the tool is institutionally approved for such data and used in accordance with applicable rules. Check with the SRC staff responsible for GDPR compliance if you are unsure. Misuse of AI tools that harm students (e.g., deceptive non-disclosure, unfair assessment practices, inappropriate sharing of student data, or delegating judgment to AI) may be addressed through relevant staff procedures. See [Procedure for handling suspicion of deviation from good research practice - Medarbetarwebben](https://medarbetare.su.se/en/our-su/governance/rules--regulations/research/procedure-for-handling-suspicion-of-deviation-from-good-research-practice).

## Application of the Policy in Practice {#t-practice}

A helpful way to think about AI tools is to imagine them as a fellow colleague that is knowledgeable, fast, and overconfident — but not accountable for your students, your course, or your grading decisions.

You can ask this “colleague” for help drafting, improving clarity, or generating options. But just as with a real colleague, you remain responsible for what is communicated to students and for every academic judgement you make. The professional rules that apply to human assistance also apply here, with one important extra constraint: AI must not influence the evaluation of graded work.

### Acceptable use {#t-acceptable}

Using AI tools is generally acceptable when they function as support for your own teaching work, for example:

- Drafting and editing student-facing text (instructions, announcements, emails) that you review and take responsibility for.
- Generating examples or questions for teaching and exams, based on your course material, which you review for correctness, bias, and alignment with the course.
- Post-grade comment polishing: after you have determined a grade, use AI to improve grammar/clarity/tone in your written comments without changing the substance.

In these cases, you remain the responsible author and professional decision-maker.

### Unacceptable use {#t-unacceptable}

Problems arise when the “colleague” effectively takes over responsibilities that must remain yours, makes autonomous decisions, or when AI use undermines trust or student rights. This includes situations such as:

- Requiring students to use genAI without guidance and support in evaluating the outcome.
- Using AI to determine, recommend, justify, or adjust grades, including “second opinions” on marks, ranking students, or converting text into a score.
- Letting AI influence evaluation by asking it whether a submission is “good enough,” whether learning outcomes are met, or what grade it “deserves.”
- Using AI-detection tools to infer whether student text was AI-generated.
- Sending AI-generated feedback that you lightly edited: you wouldn’t be considered the author of this feedback.
- Outsourcing core academic judgement (e.g., supervision advice, approvals, assessment decisions) to genAI. Students want your support and feedback, not that of genAI.

In these cases, you have delegated responsibility in ways that are not compatible with fair assessment, professional accountability, or student trust. If you believe a student could benefit from having their work checked by AI, you can suggest they do so themselves.

### Transparency matters {#t-transparency}

If you use AI in student-facing communication, students should be able to understand in which way it was used. Otherwise, they may reasonably suspect that feedback is “AI-written” and lose trust because they cannot tell whether it was simple language polishing or substantive generation.

A simple disclosure is usually enough, such as:

- which tool was used, and
- for what purpose/how its output was used

Example: “I used ChatGPT 5.2 to spellcheck my comments on your essay.”

This is part of maintaining a transparent and respectful relationship with students.

### Why the strict line for graded work {#t-graded}

Under the EU AI Act, AI systems intended to evaluate learning outcomes in education are classified as high-risk, which requires special provisions and training to use in a professional environment. That’s why this policy draws a clear boundary: AI can help with wording, but it must not influence grading decisions.

Reference: EU AI Act, Annex III, point 3 (High-risk areas). ([ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/annex-3))

## About this policy {#t-about}

This policy was developed by staff at the Stockholm Resilience Centre and discussed with researchers, teachers, and students. Generative AI (Claude Opus 4.6 and ChatGPT 5.5) was used to support drafting, language editing, and text restructuring. All content was reviewed, revised, and approved by the SRC management before adoption.
