## What you can and can't do

You'll sometimes have a strong feeling that work isn't the student's own. The feeling isn't evidence. Here's what's in your hands:

**You can't:**
- Use an [[AI detector]]. SRC rules them out for now ([[rule:t-3c|teacher policy 3C]]).
- Decide that a student cheated, or whether they meant to. At SU that's for the vice-chancellor and the disciplinary board.
- Settle a suspicion on graded work yourself, for example by failing it "for AI use", lowering the grade quietly, or agreeing with the student that it stays between you.

**You can:**
- **Ask about the work, as part of assessing it.** How did you build the argument? Why this source? Ask about the work, not about the tool.
- **Check the sources.** A reference that doesn't exist, or doesn't say what's claimed, is a fact, not a feeling. (Chapter 8 has a quick way to do this.)
- **Assess the work on its merits.** If an examined part includes explaining and defending the work, and the student can't, that part doesn't meet the learning outcomes.
- **Report a suspicion** to your department's investigating officer. That's not optional; see the case below.

## The rules behind the case

- **Suspicions get reported.** The Higher Education Ordinance (chapter 10, section 9) says a well-founded suspicion of cheating must be reported promptly to the vice-chancellor. At SU, you report it to your department's **investigating officer** (*utredningsansvarig*), who files it. If you don't know who that is at SRC, ask the director of studies. The bar is objective grounds, not proof: even fairly weak facts can be enough. SU's [rules for disciplinary matters](https://medarbetare.su.se/vart-su/styrning/regelboken/utbildning/regler-och-handlaggningsordning-for-disciplinarenden) set out the procedure; the student policy points to it too ([[rule:s-3|student policy 3]]).
- **You don't judge intent.** Whether the student *meant* to mislead is exactly what the vice-chancellor and the disciplinary board assess. Neither you nor the investigating officer weighs it before reporting.
- **The investigating officer talks to the student** about what happened, not you. Your questions about the work, as part of assessing it, are fine.
- **Nothing is settled locally while a case runs.** No resubmission deals, no reduced grade. SU recommends that the examiner wait to grade until the case is closed.
- **Only examination in the syllabus counts.** A cheating case can only concern an examination element named in the course syllabus. Drafts and ungraded tasks are outside the disciplinary rules.
- **Clear rules make all of this work.** If your brief only pointed to the policy, its line ("primarily their own intellectual contribution") is hard to apply after the fact. Concrete examples in the brief help.

## While the rules are new

Many students don't yet realise that some uses of AI count as cheating. Be understanding of that, at least for now. The question is *where* that understanding goes, because on graded work you can't act on it by deciding not to report. Three places where it legitimately sits:

1. **In the report.** Say what supports the student: they disclosed the use openly, the brief only pointed to the policy, it's their first course under these rules. The vice-chancellor can close a case without action, and that's what those facts are for.
2. **In formative work.** Drafts, practice tasks and anything ungraded fall outside the disciplinary rules. There you can explain the rule, and the student learns it before it counts. That's another reason to have a practice round early in the course.
3. **Up front.** Concrete rules in the brief, gone through in the first lecture (chapter 6), are what make the "I didn't know" problem go away.

:::grey The rules and reality
Let's be honest: if every AI-written short take-home were reported, SU would drown in cases, and in practice not every teacher reports every suspicion. We can't advise you to skip a report the rules require. But two things are true at once:

- **A hunch isn't a suspicion you can report.** A reporting duty starts with *objective grounds*: a fabricated reference, a student who says they used AI against the rules, text copied from a source. "This reads like ChatGPT" isn't one, and that's most of what teachers see.
- **Most of the gap is closed by design, not by reporting.** When take-home work is practice and the decisive part is examined in the room (chapter 5), there's little on take-home work worth cheating on and little to report. That's where your effort pays off.

When you do have something concrete and aren't sure, ask SRC's investigating officer *before* you talk to the student about what happened.
:::

:::tip Document as you go
Write down what you asked and what the student said, the same day. Keep the submission and your notes. You'll need them if it goes further, and they protect the student as much as you.
:::
