## What you can and can't do

You'll sometimes have a strong feeling that work isn't the student's own. The feeling isn't evidence. Here's what's in your hands:

**You can't:**
- Use an [[AI detector]] ([[rule:t-3c|teacher policy 3C]]).
- Decide that a student cheated. In Sweden, that's for the university's disciplinary board.
- Settle a well-founded suspicion informally, for example by lowering the grade quietly.

**You can:**
- **Talk to the student about the work.** How did you build the argument? Why this source? What would you change? Ask about the work, not about the tool.
- **Check the sources.** A reference that doesn't exist, or doesn't say what's claimed, is a fact, not a feeling.
- **Assess the work on its merits.** If the assessment includes explaining and defending it, and the student can't, it doesn't meet the learning outcomes.
- **Report a well-founded suspicion.** That's not optional; see the case below.

## The rules behind the case

- **You must report well-founded suspicions.** The Higher Education Ordinance (chapter 10, section 2) says suspected cheating that is well founded must be reported to the vice-chancellor. At SU this goes to the disciplinary board. The student's rights and the procedure are described on SU's [rights and responsibilities page](https://www.su.se/english/education/student-support/your-rights-and-responsibilities). The student policy points there too ([[rule:s-3|student policy 3]]).
- **Cheating means intent to mislead.** Using AI beyond what's allowed *can* be misconduct ([[rule:s-3]]); the board decides whether it was in this case.
- **Clear rules make all of this possible.** If your brief only pointed to the policy, its line ("primarily their own intellectual contribution") is hard to apply after the fact. Concrete examples in the brief help.
- **This is why in-class examination matters.** The case only came up because the seminar showed what the written brief couldn't. Build that moment into the assessment on purpose, instead of relying on luck.

:::grey While the rules are new
Many students don't yet realise that some uses of AI count as cheating. Be understanding of that, at least for now. Cheating means *trying to mislead* the examiner, and a student who genuinely didn't understand the rule often wasn't trying to.

Our reading in practice:
- **It looks like a misunderstanding** (they disclosed the use openly, the brief only pointed to the policy, it's their first course with these rules): talk it through, explain the rule, and assess the work on its merits. That's usually not a well-founded suspicion of cheating.
- **It looks like they knew** (the rule was concrete in the brief and gone through in class, and the use was hidden): that is a well-founded suspicion, and reporting it isn't optional.
- **Not sure which?** Ask the director of studies before you decide.

Either way, you can't promise students in advance that nothing will be reported. What you can do is make the rules so clear, early, that the excuse stops applying.
:::

:::tip Document as you go
Write down what you asked and what the student said, the same day. Keep the submission and your notes. You'll need them if it goes further, and they protect the student as much as you.
:::
