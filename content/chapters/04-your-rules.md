## You decide, and you say it up front

The teacher policy gives you, and especially the course coordinator or examiner, a clear mandate: you may **limit, expand or specify** how students use AI, for the whole course or per assignment. [[rule:t-scope]]

Two conditions:

- It must be **communicated clearly**: in the syllabus, the assignment brief or the assessment instructions.
- It must be consistent with university rules.

If you say nothing, the default student policy applies. That's legal, but vague, and vague is where the arguments start.

:::rule The most important thing in this course
Decide your AI rules **before the course starts**, write them into the syllabus and every brief, and say them out loud in the first lecture. Students who were never told clearly can't fairly be failed for breaking the rules.
:::

===

## Start from what students must do on their own

Don't start from "how much AI do I allow?". Start from the learning outcome: **what must a student be able to do without help to pass this?**

- If the point of the policy brief is that they can *build an argument from the literature*, then AI writing the argument defeats the assignment. AI checking their language doesn't.
- If the point of the modelling exercise is *interpreting results*, AI writing the R code may be fine, as long as they can explain every line.
- If the point of the seminar reflection is *engaging with the reading*, AI summarising the reading for them skips the learning.

Different assessments can have different levels. A strict line on the exam lets you be generous on formative work, where AI as a tutor can genuinely help.

:::beginner Formative vs summative?
[[Formative]] work is for learning (practice, drafts, feedback, low stakes). [[Summative]] work is what the grade is based on. Most courses can be relaxed about AI in the first and need to be clear and careful in the second.
:::

## Two honest caveats

:::grey You can't police levels 0 to 2 on take-home work
If students write at home, you can't check whether they used AI "only for language". The rules still matter, because they make the expectation clear and give you grounds when work clearly isn't the student's own. But the real protection is assessment design: drafts, a short oral follow-up, in-class work, tasks built on local material. That's the next chapter.
:::

:::grey Disclosure is necessary, not sufficient
Ask for disclosure at every level that allows AI. But a student who discloses forbidden use has still broken the rule ([[rule:s-2|student policy 2]]). Say that explicitly, or some students will treat disclosure as a permission slip.
:::

What makes rules stick, in our experience: students know **why** the line is where it is. That's what the "on their own" field is for.
