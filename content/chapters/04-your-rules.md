## Start from the rules we have

SRC's student policy is the default for every course. For most teachers, the best move is to **keep it and make it concrete**: say for each assignment what it means in practice, and why.

- **The teacher policy is the same for everyone.** You can't loosen or tighten it for your course.
- **The student rules can be adapted per course or assignment** ([[rule:t-scope]]). That's meant for teachers who know these tools well and have a clear reason. If you're not sure, don't. A badly adapted rule is worse than the default.
- **Whatever you choose, write it down and say it.** In the syllabus, in every brief, and in the first lecture.

:::rule The most important thing in this chapter
Have your AI rules ready **before the course starts**, and go through them with students in the first session. Students who were never told clearly can't fairly be failed for breaking the rules.
:::

===

## What must students do on their own?

The default rule is that AI may support learning but not replace the work being assessed. To make that concrete, ask: **what must a student be able to do without help to pass this?**

- In a **policy brief**, maybe *building an argument from the literature*. AI explaining a concept is fine; AI drafting the argument isn't.
- In a **modelling exercise**, maybe *interpreting results*. AI explaining an error message is fine; AI writing the analysis isn't.
- In a **seminar reflection**, *engaging with the reading*. AI quizzing them on it is fine; AI summarising it for them skips the point.

Tell students that answer. Rules they understand get followed more than rules they don't.

:::beginner Formative vs summative?
[[Formative]] work is for learning (practice, drafts, feedback, low stakes). [[Summative]] work is what the grade is based on. AI as a tutor helps most in the first; the second is where you need to see what the student can do.
:::

## Make it concrete for each assessment

For each of your assessments: see what the SRC rules mean in practice, add what students must be able to do on their own, and copy the text for the brief. If you're experienced with AI and have a reason to change the rules for one assessment, you can choose "Adapt".

## Two things to be honest about

:::grey You won't be able to tell who followed the rules
On take-home work, under these rules, unauthorised AI use is very hard to spot. You can't use detectors, and good AI text doesn't look like AI. The rules still matter: they set expectations and give you grounds when work clearly isn't the student's own. But they don't let you *check*. That's what in-class examination is for (next chapter).
:::

:::grey Disclosure is necessary, not sufficient
Ask students to disclose meaningful AI use. But a student who discloses forbidden use has still broken the rule ([[rule:s-2|student policy 2]]). Say that explicitly, or some will treat disclosure as a permission slip.
:::
